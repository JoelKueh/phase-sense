  \begin{block}{Emergent Phenomena}
    Cambridge Dictionary - ``An emergent property of a complex system is one that does not belong to any part of that system on its own, but that happens as a result of parts of the system interacting''. Emergent phenomena are everywhere, and the analysis of emergent phenomena is useful in many fields.

    For example, understanding the aggregation kinetics of asbestos particles suspended in water can help researchers better understand how asbestos spreads in wet environments (see Figure~\ref{fig:emergence} below). Being able to predict the onset of HTT (Huntingtin) protein aggregation in neurons under a microscope is important for research on neurodegenerative diseases such as Huntington's disease~\cite{htt}.

    \begin{figure}[h]
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{emergence.jpg}
      \caption{Emergence curves for the aggregation of asbestos particles~\cite{aggr}.}
      \label{fig:emergence}
    \end{figure}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Neural Networks for Emergence Analysis}
    Analysis of emergent phenomena can be difficult, and classical computer vision techniques can only go so far. Low resolutions, small particle sizes, and lens aberrations all complicate conventional analysis. Neural networks seem a good alternative, but it is often hard to source large, annotated datasets to train them. Our project demonstrates that neural networks trained on simulated footage are a suitable alternative to classical techniques in the analysis of emergent phenomena in microscopy.

    \begin{enumerate}
      \item \textbf{PyTorch Neural Network} --- Analyzes time-lapse microscope video of emergent phenomena.
      \item \textbf{Digital Twin Simulator} --- Creates annotated footage that serves as an unlimited training and testing set for the neural network.
      \item \textbf{Computer Vision Pipeline} --- Analyzes simulated video using classical computer vision techniques. Serves as a baseline for our neural network.
    \end{enumerate}
  \end{block}

\end{column}

\begin{column}{\colwidth}

  \begin{block}{Methodology}
    We chose to model and analyze a similar physical phenomenon to that investigated in the paper ``Aggregation of Elongated Colloids in Water''~\cite{aggr}. We implemented this as a Brownian motion simulation with random accelerations and probabilistic aggregation.

    The digital twin generates hundreds of simulated microscope videos with randomized particle shapes, accelerations, aggregation probabilities, and onset times. An example frame is in Figure~\ref{fig:frame} below.

    \begin{figure}[h]
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.57\textwidth]{frame.png}
      \caption{Sample image from our simulated microscope footage.}
      \label{fig:frame}
    \end{figure}

    The core of our PyTorch neural network is a CNN-TCN (Convolutional Neural Network, Temporal Convolutional Network) hybrid model. The CNN (built on ResNet50) extracts spatial information from the frames of the video, while the TCN extracts temporal information over a five-frame window. The final layer produces a single emergence index for each frame. We stitch these indices together into curves and use thresholding to determine the aggregation onset.

    Using Canny edge detection and a morphological closing operation, the computer vision pipeline identifies clusters of particles as connected groups of pixels. The model then computes the emergence index as the average number of white pixels in each cluster. The onset is computed in real time via thresholding. Figure~\ref{fig:curve} below contains a comparison of normalized emergence curves from the digital twin and the pipelines.

    \begin{figure}[h]
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{result_norm.png}
      \caption{Normalized comparison of emergence curves.}
      \label{fig:curve}
    \end{figure}
  \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Results}
    The machine learning model and computer vision models both produce reasonable emergence curves and onset estimates. Normalized cumulative error and onset error histograms are below.

    \begin{figure}[h]
      \centering
      \includegraphics[width=0.63\textwidth]{result_tw_minmax_error.png}
      \includegraphics[width=0.63\textwidth]{result_onset_error.png}
      \caption{Cumulative error and onset error distributions for the pipelines.}
      \label{fig:onset}
    \end{figure}
   \end{block}

  \begin{block}{Conclusions}
    Both pipelines performed very well. The machine learning model performed almost as well as the computer vision pipeline in onset detection and had a smaller cumulative emergence error. There is a lot more testing that could be done with this approach, however.

    Future work should make use of the generality of machine learning models. Testing the ability of models to infer physical parameters (e.g., determining particle aggregation probability directly from video) or to analyze more complicated physical phenomena could further emphasize the benefits that machine learning models can have.
  \end{block}
